## Purpose and Scope of the Work Permit System

A work permit is a written authorisation issued before non-routine work begins in
an operating area of a hydrocarbon installation. Its purpose is to ensure that the
hazards of the job have been identified, that the equipment has been positively
isolated and made safe, and that the issuing authority and the performing authority
share the same understanding of the conditions under which work may proceed.

Permits are issued for a defined job, a defined location, and a defined validity
period — typically one shift. A permit does not carry over a shift change without
revalidation, because the conditions it certified may no longer hold and the
people who certified them are no longer present.

Common permit categories are hot work, cold work, confined space entry, electrical
isolation, excavation, and work at height. Where a job falls into more than one
category, every applicable permit is required.

## Hot Work — Definition and Ignition Control

Hot work is any activity capable of producing a source of ignition: welding,
cutting, grinding, brazing, soldering, open flame, or the use of non-intrinsically-safe
equipment in a classified area.

The controlling principle is that an ignition source must never be introduced where
a flammable atmosphere may be present. Because a flammable atmosphere can develop
after a permit is issued — from a fresh leak, from disturbed ventilation, or from
work happening nearby — the gas condition is a continuing requirement, not a
one-time check at issue.

Hot work must be stopped if the combustible gas concentration rises during the job.
Resumption requires a fresh gas test and revalidation of the permit.

## Hot Work — Gas Testing Requirements

Gas testing is performed by a competent person using a calibrated instrument
immediately before the permit is issued, and repeated periodically while the work
continues.

Combustible gas is measured as a percentage of the Lower Explosive Limit (% LEL).
The acceptance criterion for hot work is stringent: the reading must be at or near
zero, and well below the level at which a conventional first alarm would sound.
A rising trend is itself disqualifying even when the instantaneous reading is
within limits, because the trend indicates an uncontrolled source.

Testing must be performed at the work location and at points where heavier-than-air
gas may accumulate — low points, pits, trenches, and enclosed spaces adjacent to the
job. A single reading at a fixed detector is not sufficient evidence that the work
area is safe, particularly where ventilation has been disturbed.

## Confined Space Entry — Atmospheric Requirements

Entry into a confined space requires testing for three conditions before entry and
at intervals during occupancy:

- **Oxygen content** must be within the normal breathable range. Both oxygen
  deficiency and oxygen enrichment are disqualifying; enrichment sharply increases
  fire risk.
- **Combustible gas** must be at or near zero % LEL.
- **Toxic contaminants** must be below the permissible exposure limit for the
  substance concerned.

Testing is performed at the top, middle and bottom of the space, since gases
stratify by density. Continuous monitoring is required where conditions may change.
A standby person remains outside the space throughout the entry, and rescue
arrangements are in place before entry begins.

## Suspension, Cancellation and Revalidation

A permit is suspended or cancelled when the conditions it certified no longer hold.
Triggers include a rise in combustible gas, failure or disturbance of ventilation,
an alarm in the area, an emergency elsewhere on the site, or the expiry of the
validity period.

Suspension is the safe default. The burden is on re-establishing safe conditions
and revalidating, not on demonstrating that the changed condition is harmless.

Where an automated system detects a condition that would invalidate the permit, it
may withhold or suspend authorisation. Such a system must never be able to grant
authorisation that the gas and oxygen criteria have refused.

## Simultaneous and Adjacent Operations

Hazards combine. Maintenance work that opens a system, hot work nearby, and a shift
changeover that thins supervision are individually routine and jointly dangerous.

The permit system requires that the issuing authority consider work already
authorised in the same area and in adjacent areas. Where activities interact, the
permits must be cross-referenced and the combined hazard assessed, rather than each
job being assessed only against its own scope.

Particular attention is required when maintenance disturbs ventilation while an
ignition source is authorised nearby, since the fixed gas detector may under-read
the true concentration at the work face.

## Shift Handover and Continuity of Control

Responsibility for an open permit transfers at shift change through a documented
handover. The incoming issuing authority satisfies themselves that the conditions
certified by the permit still hold.

Handover is a recognised point of vulnerability. Information is lost, attention is
divided, and the incoming shift lacks the context that made an anomaly meaningful to
the outgoing shift. Permits that would otherwise be revalidated may be carried over
by assumption.

## Roles and Competence

The **issuing authority** owns the plant and certifies the conditions.
The **performing authority** owns the job and accepts the conditions.
The **gas tester** must be trained and using an instrument within calibration.

Each role is held by a named, competent individual. Authority is not delegated to a
system. Automation may inform, escalate, or veto — the accountable decision to
permit work remains with a person.
